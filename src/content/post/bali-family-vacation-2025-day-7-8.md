## Day 7 : Back to Diving, let's go!

_Date: Thursday, Jun 19, 2025_

### My Adventures

I had an early start today because I had to travel from Ubud to Tulamben for my dives with **[Dive Concepts](https://maps.app.goo.gl/j8pFXgZVY4XfRR8w7)**. They arranged a pick-up and the driver, Yuta, arrived at Junna House by 4:45 AM. We left by 5:00 AM — Tulamben is roughly a 2½–3 hour drive from Ubud, so I napped in the car to bank a little extra sleep for later.

We reached Dive Concepts around 08:00. I met up with Pranav, a friend from a Pondicherry dive — we had chatted before the trip, and it turned out our diving dates at Tulamben and Amed ended up matching. Having a familiar face felt great.

The plan: three dives — the famous USAT Liberty shipwreck, then the Drop-Off Wall, and finally Coral Garden. I had planned ahead to get my Nitrox certification in Nusa Penida because Dive Concepts provides Nitrox free of cost to certified divers — if I had done the certification at Dive Concepts, I wouldn’t have been able to use Nitrox until the next day. After gearing up and marking the tanks, we hopped in the back of a small truck for a 5-minute ride to the shore entry.

The beach near the USAT Liberty is rocky sand — dive boots are a must or your feet will file a complaint. The shore was busier than I expected, but the diveable area is large so we didn’t feel crowded underwater. After a quick dive brief, we descended along a sloping drop toward the wreck.

The USAT Liberty started life as a US Army cargo ship and was beached at Tulamben after being torpedoed in 1942; tremors from Mount Agung’s 1963 eruption finally slid her off the sand and into the sea, where she now rests offshore. The wreck is about 120 m long and sits approximately 30–40 m from the beach; the shallowest parts rise to just a few metres below the surface while other sections descend toward ~25–30 m, which makes it accessible to many levels of certified divers. Coral has colonised much of the structure, creating dramatic swim-throughs and photo opportunities that are the wreck’s main draw.

The wreck itself was unreal — big, encrusted, and with a few rooms you can slip through. Fish life around the wreck is moderate — the structure itself is the spectacle. I was careful with my weighting this time (lesson learned in Nusa Penida), so buoyancy stayed pleasant. My dive here lasted about 53 minutes with an average depth of 16.2 m and a max depth around 27 m.

Next was the Tulamben Drop-Off: a reef that slopes into a steep wall on one side and is lined with good coral gardens on the other. Like the wreck, this was a shore entry. Currents were light — they helped on the outbound leg but made the return a little more effortful. We drifted along the wall and enjoyed the corals; the dive clocked ~52 minutes, average depth ~16.1 m, max ~28.2 m.

Meanwhile, my family had a slower morning. Their plan: a rice terrace Kusuma recommended, then Pura Tirta Empul, then Goa Gajah — with flexibility depending on how tired everyone felt.

After breakfast they went to a nearby **[Rice Terrace](https://maps.app.goo.gl/x75t7fkwSM5JK2ge8)**. This kind of terrace farming arranges paddies in cascading steps along hillsides; locally the subak irrigation system creates the distinctive layered look. The terraces are a short drive north of Ubud and are known for dramatic green views, sunrise photo spots, and tourist amenities like swings and small cafes — nice to wander.

Tirta Empul is an ancient water temple (founded around the 10th century) famous for its holy spring used for purification rituals; visitors can witness or take part in the ritual baths where water pours from a line of spouts into a communal pool. There are rules for entry and modest dress is expected.The temple remains an active site of worship and a fascinating insight into Balinese Hindu practice.

They spent about 30 minutes here, which was enough to see the main areas without getting into the water. Before entering Goa Gajah they were hungry and tried an Indian place nearby — the meal was disappointing, so they abandoned it and pressed on.

Goa Gajah (the “Elephant Cave”) is an archaeological site dating back to around the 9th–11th centuries; it features a carved stone entrance with a menacing face, a small cave with Hindu and Buddhist iconography, and bathing pools that were excavated in the 1950s. The cave is atmospheric but compact — there’s a fair amount of walking and steps to reach the main spot. The surrounding terraces and souvenir stalls make it a quick stop for most visitors. They spent about 45 minutes to an hour here, which was enough to explore the cave and the surrounding area.

For them, Goa Gajah was a little underwhelming (small cave, lots of stairs) but the souvenir stalls at the entrance had great prices compared to the markets — they regretted not buying more before the Ubud markets the next day. They returned to Junna House with mixed feelings, picked at fruit and snacks from India, and called it an early night.

## Day 8 : Onwards to my 50th Dive

_Date: Friday, Jun 20, 2025_

### My Adventures

Today I dove in **Amed**, a quieter coastal town near Tulamben. I packed and planned to head back to Ubud after the dives. The three shore dives scheduled were Jemuluk Wall, Jemuluk Bay, and Pyramid Point.

The Jemuluk Bay dive clocked ~58 minutes, avg depth ~11.6 m, max ~19.8 m.

Meanwhile, my family’s day was shopping-focused. They planned to visit the **[Ubud Palace](https://maps.app.goo.gl/XA7LUDTCLVuLpAQs8)** and **[Pura Taman Kemuda Saraswati (Water Palace)](https://maps.app.goo.gl/Ch1Vq7koCXb5Tx7i7)** before the markets.

<figure class="flex flex-col items-center mx-auto md:w-3/4">
  <img src="/assets/images/posts/bali-family-vacation-2025/day-7-8/8_8.png" />
  <figcaption class="text-center text-sm text-gray-600 dark:text-gray-400 mt-2">
    My aunt at the Water Palace
  </figcaption>
</figure>

[Pura Taman Kemuda Saraswati](https://maps.app.goo.gl/Ch1Vq7koCXb5Tx7i7) was built in the early 1950s and was designed by I Gusti Nyoman Lempad; it’s dedicated to Dewi Saraswati, the goddess of knowledge and the arts, and is best known for its ornate carvings and lotus pond — picturesque and calm, but mostly a photo spot rather than a long cultural stop. The temple’s outer grounds and lotus pond are very photogenic, and visitors often rent traditional attire there for pictures.

<figure class="flex flex-col items-center mx-auto">
  <img src="/assets/images/posts/bali-family-vacation-2025/day-7-8/8_9.png" />
  <figcaption class="text-center text-sm text-gray-600 dark:text-gray-400 mt-2">
    Selfie at the Ubud Palace
  </figcaption>
</figure>

[Ubud Palace](https://maps.app.goo.gl/XA7LUDTCLVuLpAQs8) (Puri Saren Agung) is the historic royal compound in the heart of Ubud; the public areas are free to visit during the day, and each evening the palace stage hosts traditional dance performances (tickets are usually sold for the shows). For my family it felt like a photo opportunity more than an immersive cultural experience, and they preferred the markets for the real fun.

After palace hopping they spent a long time bargaining at the Ubud Markets for clothes, bags, and little trinkets. Prices start high but come down with patient (and mildly theatrical) bargaining — they did better here than they later did in Kuta.

By about 15:00 they were exhausted and hungry; a stop at **[Swagath Indian Restaurant](https://maps.app.goo.gl/mcXXpFnv7vzmSm468)** on the way back hit the spot. Full, tired, and satisfied, they returned to Junna House.

  <figcaption class="text-center text-sm text-gray-600 dark:text-gray-400 mt-2">
    Our Room at Junna House
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The host at Junna House offered Balinese massages and my family couldn’t resist — they said it was strong, energizing, and cheap: a very welcome end to the day.

When I returned after two days of diving we swapped stories, ate dinner from Pepito market supplies, and relaxed. It felt good to be back together — and to close this chapter of the trip on a high note (and with 50 dives logged!).

Our Bali trip was winding down, but these two days gave us a perfect mix: underwater thrills for me, easy cultural strolling for them — everyone got a win.
